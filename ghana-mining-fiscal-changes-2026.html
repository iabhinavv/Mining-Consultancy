Ghana's 2026 mining fiscal changes: the full register

Four fiscal changes took effect in Ghana this year and the Minerals Commission ran its first sector audit in a decade. Here is each one, what it replaced, and what it changes in your ledger.

If you run finance for a mining group in Ghana, 2026 has been the busiest fiscal year in a decade. This page is the register we work from. It is published in full, with sources, because the analysis is only useful if you can check it.

Change In force
Sliding-scale mineral royalty (L.I. 2517)10 Mar 2026
Growth and Sustainability Levy cut, 3% to 1%31 Mar 2026
Mandatory e-invoicing for VAT-registered businesses1 Jul 2026
GRA Integrated Tax Administration System rolloutSep 2026
Minerals Commission physical and financial auditNov 2025 – Jun 2026

1. The flat 5% royalty is gone

The Minerals and Mining (Royalties) Regulations, 2025 (L.I. 2517) came into force on 10 March 2026 and abolished the fixed 5% rate along with the previous royalty bands for gold and lithium.

Gold now pays on a sliding scale tied to the price: a minimum of 5% where the gold price is below $2,000 an ounce, rising to a maximum of 12% where the price exceeds $4,500 an ounce. The structure is progressive, so state revenue rises with the commodity price.

Lithium moved to its own scale, by spodumene price per tonne:

  • 5% — up to US$1,500 per tonne
  • 7% — US$1,501 to US$2,500
  • 10% — US$2,501 to US$3,000
  • 12% — above US$3,000

What it changes in your ledger. The royalty line stops being a constant. Any model, accrual, budget or covenant calculation that carries 5% as a hard-coded figure is now wrong, and wrong in the direction of understating cost whenever the price is high. It also creates a correlation you did not previously have to manage: the same price move that lifts revenue lifts your royalty rate.

2. The Growth and Sustainability Levy came down

Parliament passed the Growth and Sustainability Levy (Amendment) Act, 2026, reducing the GSL on the gold mining sector from 3% to 1%, with effect from 31 March 2026. The stated rationale was to offset the higher fiscal burden created by the expanded royalty scale.

What it changes in your ledger. Two changes now move in opposite directions within three weeks of each other. Whether your group is better or worse off depends on your realised price and your profitability, and the only honest way to answer it is to recalculate both lines across the periods affected rather than estimate the net.

Note the dates carefully: a period straddling 10 and 31 March has to be split, and the two changes do not share a boundary.

3. E-invoicing became mandatory

From 1 July 2026, the GRA enforces mandatory electronic invoicing for all VAT-registered businesses. Invoices must be generated through GRA-certified e-invoicing software and transmitted in real time to the GRA's central validation platform before being issued to the customer.

Two consequences deserve attention. First, the e-invoice is the only valid tax document for claiming input tax credit. Second, businesses that failed to onboard by 30 June 2026 face automatic suspension of their VAT registration pending compliance.

What it changes in your ledger. This is a working capital risk wearing an IT costume. If a supplier is not onboarded, or your own onboarding is incomplete, input tax recovery is at risk — and for a capital-intensive operation that is real cash. It is worth testing your largest suppliers' status, not only your own.

4. GRA replaced its tax administration platform

The GRA has launched an Integrated Tax Administration System (ITAS) replacing the Taxpayer's Portal and the Ghana Integrated Tax Management and Information System (GITMIS), with national rollout set for September 2026.

What it changes in your ledger. Nothing directly, but platform migrations are where filing histories, credits and carried-forward balances go missing. Reconcile what the new system says you owe against your own records before you accept it, particularly for refunds in progress.

5. The first sector audit in a decade

Ghana ordered a nationwide physical and financial audit of mining firms running from 1 November 2025 to June 2026. Miners were required to submit ten years of production logs, three years of financial records, and all permits.

What it changes in your ledger. Again, nothing directly — but it is a reporting stress test, and it told a lot of groups something uncomfortable about how much of their reporting lives in one spreadsheet and one person's head. If producing three years of financial records at short notice was difficult, that is a consolidation problem, and it will recur.

What to change, in order

  1. Recalculate royalty for every period since 10 March 2026 against the scale actually in force, using your realised prices. Compare to what you filed.
  2. Split the GSL periods at 31 March and confirm the rate applied either side.
  3. Rebuild the royalty line in your cash and budget models as a function of the price assumption rather than a fixed percentage.
  4. Test e-invoicing status for your own entities and your largest suppliers, and quantify input tax at risk.
  5. Reconcile your GRA position after the ITAS migration, especially refunds in progress.
  6. Review covenant headroom under the new effective rate at two or three price scenarios.
