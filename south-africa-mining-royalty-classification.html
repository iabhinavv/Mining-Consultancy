Refined or unrefined? The classification that sets your royalty

South Africa's mineral royalty is not one calculation but two, and which one applies to your output is a classification decision. Get it wrong and the error repeats every period, across the whole stream.

Ghana spent 2026 changing its mining fiscal rules. South Africa did not — and that is precisely why the money here is lost differently. The exposure is not new legislation to react to; it is a technical regime where a single classification decision quietly compounds.

Two formulas, two caps

Under the Mineral and Petroleum Resources Royalty Act, 2008, a royalty is payable on the transfer of mineral resources extracted in South Africa. The royalty is formula-driven, based on the extractor's gross sales, with the rate derived from earnings before interest and tax divided by gross sales.

There are two formulas, and they differ in both their denominator and their ceiling:

Classification Minimum rate Maximum rate
Refined mineral resources0.5%5%
Unrefined mineral resources0.5%7%

Two percentage points of cap, on gross sales, is not a rounding difference. On a mid-tier operation it is routinely the largest single recoverable item a review finds.

Where groups get it wrong

The classification is a question about the condition of the mineral at the point it is transferred, not about what the mine calls its product internally or how the sales contract is titled. Three patterns recur:

  • Inheriting a treatment nobody re-tested. A classification decided years ago, when the processing route was different, carried forward because nothing prompted a review.
  • Treating the whole operation as one stream. Where an operation produces both refined and unrefined output, these are not one position.
  • Letting the sales contract decide. The commercial description of the product and the statutory classification are different questions and do not always agree.

Because the error applies to the whole stream for the whole period, it does not announce itself. It looks like a consistent royalty rate, which is exactly what you would expect to see if everything were correct.

The apportionment problem

Where a miner has both refined and unrefined mineral resources, earnings before interest and tax must be calculated separately for each group. That requires allowable expenses shared between the two categories to be apportioned.

This is the second layer, and in practice the messier one. Shared processing costs, shared overheads and shared logistics all have to be split on a basis that is defensible rather than convenient — and because EBIT drives the rate in both formulas, the apportionment basis directly moves the royalty payable on both streams.

Gross sales and market value

Gross sales must reflect the market value of the mineral. Where connected-party charges are deducted in arriving at gross sales — freight, insurance, handling — those charges need to be on arm's length terms and properly evidenced.

For any group that sells through an offshore marketing or trading entity, this is the most likely place for a royalty adjustment to arise, and the records required to defend it are the records nobody keeps until they are asked for them.

How to test your own position

You can do most of this internally before engaging anyone:

  1. Write down why each output stream is classified as it is, and find the document that supports it. If nobody can produce one, that is your answer about when it was last tested.
  2. Check whether you have both. If any part of the operation produces refined output and any part unrefined, confirm EBIT is being determined separately.
  3. Look at your apportionment basis for shared expenses and ask whether you would defend it in writing to SARS.
  4. Reconcile gross sales to market value, and list every connected-party deduction with the evidence supporting its arm's length basis.
  5. Recalculate one period both ways. The difference between the two formulas on a single period tells you what the question is worth before you spend anything answering it.

If step 5 produces a number large enough to matter, that is the point at which an independent recalculation pays for itself — see royalty and tax compliance or our South Africa page, which also covers section 36(7F) capex ring-fencing and carbon tax at R308 a tonne.
